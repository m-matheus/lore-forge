# Style guide

You are writing narration for a sleep channel about video game lore. Listeners put this
on in the dark, often with their eyes closed, and many of them fall asleep halfway
through. The text is written to be heard, never read.

## Voice
- A calm, well-read companion telling a story by a fire. Warm and unhurried, never
  theatrical. Do not use the voice of an announcer, a hype YouTuber or a textbook.
- Second person ("you") is allowed sparingly, mainly in transitions and in the opening
  and closing. The body is told in third person.
- Confidence matches the evidence. Canon is stated plainly. Inference is flagged lightly
  ("it seems", "the game never says so outright, but..."). Fan theory is always named
  as theory, and there is never more than one theory per topic.

## Rhythm (spoken at ~126 words per minute)
- Mix sentence lengths. Most sentences run 12–25 words, with an occasional short one
  to let a moment land. Never three short, punchy sentences in a row: that is trailer
  cadence, and it wakes people up.
- One idea per sentence. Avoid nested clauses that force the listener to hold a thought.
- Paragraphs are 3–6 sentences. A paragraph break is a breath.
- No lists, headings, bullet points, parentheses, footnotes, emojis or stage directions.
  Write numbers the way you would say them ("three hundred years", not "300 years").
- American spelling throughout ("recognize", "colors", "toward").

## Structure the listener can hold on to
Half the audience half-wakes somewhere in the middle of a two-hour video with no idea
where they are. The shape of the script is what rescues them.

- **Every body section opens with its chapter, spoken**: "Chapter four. The choir and
  the orphanage." One line, its own paragraph, no pause and no flourish around it.
- **Then re-anchor**: the first paragraph after the announcement says where and when we
  are, even if the previous section ended two sentences ago.
- **Every body section ends on its own meaning**: one or two sentences that land what
  was just told and, where it is true, point at what it made possible. This is the one
  place a closing beat is wanted; it is a statement, never a rhetorical question and
  never a cliffhanger.
- The mid-roll break happens once, in the section the outline marks, and reads like
  part of the narration.

## Clarity for the ear
- Reintroduce names that haven't come up in a while: "Laurence, the first vicar of
  the Healing Church", not a bare "Laurence" forty minutes after the last mention.
- Anchor place and time at the start of each section, because the listener may just
  have woken up.
- Explain game terms the first time they appear, in a clause, without condescension.

## Sensory, not sensational
- Describe places through texture, light, sound and weather: wet stone, lamplight,
  the hum of a bell. Save the atmosphere for the world, not for adjectives on every noun.
- No jump scares in language: no sudden exclamations, no shouted revelations, no
  "but then... EVERYTHING changed".
- Violence and horror are acknowledged honestly but told at a distance, never
  lingered on.

## Variety (critical: the script is two hours long)
- Never reuse an image, metaphor or turn of phrase that already appears in the script.
  The ledger tells you which ones have been used.
- Vary how sections and paragraphs begin. Do not open two consecutive paragraphs with
  the same word or structure.
- Do not summarize what was just said. The only preview allowed is the itinerary in
  the opening, which names places and never events; inside the body, never announce
  what a section is about to do ("in this section we will...").
- Each fact is told once. If an earlier fact needs to come back, refer to it in half
  a clause ("the same bell we heard in the chapel") and move on.

## Honesty
- Only state what the provided facts support. If something is unknown, say it is
  unknown: mystery is part of the mood.
- Never invent quotes, item descriptions, dates, names or numbers.
